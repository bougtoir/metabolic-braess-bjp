# Abstract

Guild-level contrasts in beta diversity — for example, whether
predators turn over in space more or less than their prey — are
increasingly used to infer spatial ecology in fossil assemblages. But
fossil assemblages differ structurally between guilds in gamma
diversity, dominance, taxonomic resolution, temporal averaging and
sampling. We quantify how strongly these properties distort guild
contrasts using a simulation framework with known ground truth
(Δβ_true = 0) plus an empirical decomposition of the Upper Jurassic
Morrison Formation dinosaur record. With identical underlying spatial
processes, a realistic four-taxon predator pool against a 26-taxon
herbivore pool produced an apparent contrast bias of ≈ −0.17 (extreme
two-taxon scenario ≈ −0.41); a single dominant taxon and genus-level
lumping added further apparent continuity; temporal pooling drove
observed contrasts toward zero, erasing structure rather than creating
it; and factorial simulations mapped joint sign-reversal and
false-difference regions. In Morrison, a naive contrast of Δβ =
−0.550 was progressively attenuated by structural correction — falling
to −0.020 after removing the dominant theropod Allosaurus — and an
identical pipeline on the Nemegt Formation found no such contrast
(Δβ = +0.094). Fossil assemblage construction can thus both create
and erase apparent ecological spatial structure; we propose a
diagnostic workflow that should precede any ecological interpretation
of guild-level fossil beta diversity.
